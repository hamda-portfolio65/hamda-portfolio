Project 1

What 3,900 Customers Reveal About Pricing, Stock and Loyalty

Retail Customer Analytics · February to March 2026 Four-person university project. Dataset and chart outputs were shared across the team.
Tools Used

Techniques Used
Descriptive Statistics · Pivot-Table Segmentation Analysis · Data-Quality Check on Headline Figures · Predictive Model Specification
Project Description

An analysis of 3,900 retail customer records covering $233,081 of sales, examining what the patterns mean for pricing, stock planning, marketing spend and customer retention. The dataset combines categorical and numeric variables including payment method, revenue, product and time, which supports statistical analysis alongside visualisation and preprocessing.

Key Findings
  1. Clothing carries the business, generating $104,264 (45% of all revenue) and leading in every season. So what: clothing stock and marketing spend should stay the priority category year round.
  2. Male customers generate more total revenue than female customers, but average spend per customer is almost identical ($59.54 against $60.25). The gap is a headcount gap, not a spending gap. So what: closing the gender headcount gap is an acquisition opportunity worth roughly $80,000 at current spend rates, not a targeting one.
  3. Seasonal variation is mild: only an 8% gap between the strongest season (Fall) and the weakest (Summer). So what: large seasonal stock swings are not justified, except for clothing's summer dip.
  4. Age does not predict spend. Average spend sits within a $1.40 range across every age bracket. So what: age is a weak basis for segmentation; loyalty band varies far more meaningfully.
  5. 43% of revenue comes from discounted sales, with almost no difference in average basket size between discounted and full-price transactions. So what: this is the single biggest open margin question in the data, addressed directly in the discount-need model below.
A Check on the Headline Numbers

Dividing revenue by customer count for every segment (gender, age bracket, and full price against discounted) shows average spend sits between $58.95 and $60.31 throughout, a spread of under $1.40. Every revenue difference elsewhere in the analysis is a volume difference, not a spending difference, which redirects where effort should go: more customers and more repeat visits, not a bigger basket.

Predictive Models Proposed

Four models were specified (not built) to move from reporting what happened to anticipating what customers will do next: a regression estimating how much a customer will spend, a regression estimating how often they will buy, a classifier predicting who is likely to subscribe, and a classifier predicting who actually needs a discount to convert.

Recommendations
  1. Test the discount strategy before scaling it back. Build the discount-need model first, then adjust pricing on evidence rather than the headline 43% figure.
  2. Keep seasonal stock adjustments modest. Plan around the one real movement: clothing dipping in summer.
  3. Build a top loyalty tier for the highest purchase-frequency band. Those customers are the most expensive to replace.
  4. Treat the gender revenue gap as an acquisition target, not a targeting one. Both groups already spend the same per head.
Project 2

Can a Distributed Ledger Fix Record Sharing Across a Five-Hospital NHS Trust?

Blockchain Feasibility Study · Barts Health NHS Trust · May to June 2026 Four-person university project. Research inputs were shared across the team.
Tools Used
Feasibility Study & Options Appraisal · Architecture Decision Records · Hyperledger Besu (Proof of Authority) · Risk Register · Requirements Catalogue · Stakeholder Analysis · Platform Comparison
Techniques Used
Feasibility Assessment · MoSCoW Requirements Prioritisation · Stakeholder Analysis · Options Appraisal · Risk Assessment · Gap Analysis
Project Description

Barts Health NHS Trust runs five hospitals serving more than 2.6 million people, and cannot reliably share patient records between its own sites. Each hospital has historically run a separate record system, producing treatment delays, repeated diagnostic tests and medication errors, alongside a demonstrated security exposure and no dependable way of recording patient consent or access. This desk-based feasibility study, using published sources, assesses whether distributed ledger technology fixes that, and on what architecture.

Key Findings
  1. Decentralisation removes the single point of failure that a documented 2017 ransomware attack and a later national data exposure both exploited. Limitation: resilience still depends on having enough participating nodes; a small consortium remains exposed.
  2. Immutability and auditability produce a tamper-evident record of every access decision, which the trust currently cannot produce at all. Limitation: blockchain guarantees integrity, not correctness. An incorrect record cannot be deleted, only separately amended.
  3. Transparency lets authorised participants, potentially including patients, see complete access logs. Limitation: transparency reaches only consortium members, and wider membership adds governance obligations.
  4. Interoperability works as an integration layer over the existing hospital systems rather than replacing them. Limitation: it does not reconcile different clinical coding systems between hospitals; that depends on shared data standards being adopted separately.
The Consent Process

Every record is linked to a unique Patient ID, and three checks run in sequence before access is granted: is the requester a verified clinician on the network, has the patient given active consent to this clinician, and does the request fall within the permitted scope. If all three pass, access is granted and logged to the blockchain; if any fails, access is denied and the failed attempt is logged too.

IF a verified doctor requests access AND the patient gives consent AND the request is within allowed scope, THEN the contract logs the authorisation and the event is recorded on the blockchain. ELSE the request is denied.

Recommendation

A private, permissioned network on Hyperledger Besu using Proof of Authority consensus, with patient data held off-chain in the existing systems and only hashes committed to the ledger. Public networks are unsuitable for identifiable healthcare data.

  1. Validator selection is the critical control. Under Proof of Authority, network security reduces to the trustworthiness of the validator set, a governance decision needed before any technical work starts.
  2. The emergency override must be designed and governed before deployment, or the efficiency benefit is reversed in urgent cases rather than simply absent.
  3. Legal advice is needed on whether a hash itself counts as personal data under GDPR before relying on the off-chain mitigation.
The caveat that matters most: the consent contract verifies that a requester is a network member. It cannot judge whether their reason is legitimate. Blockchain makes misconduct traceable, not impossible.
